## El pasaje — *Learning from Nature: Biomimicry in Engineering*

### Párrafo A

Biomimicry is the practice of studying biological structures and processes in order to solve human design problems. The term was coined in the late 1990s by the American biologist Janine Benyus, although the underlying approach is considerably older: Leonardo da Vinci's sketches of flying machines drew directly on the anatomy of birds, and nineteenth-century architects studied the ribbed structure of water lilies when designing glass roofs. What distinguishes contemporary biomimicry from these isolated precedents is its systematic character. Rather than borrowing a single form, engineers now consult biological databases in the same way they might consult a catalogue of materials.

### Párrafo B

Perhaps the most frequently cited case concerns the Japanese Shinkansen. Early versions of the high-speed train produced a loud boom each time they emerged from a tunnel, as compressed air was released suddenly into the open. The engineer responsible for redesigning the nose was a keen birdwatcher, and he noted that the kingfisher enters water almost without a splash, its long tapered beak displacing the liquid gradually rather than all at once. The redesigned nose adopted the same geometry. The noise problem disappeared, and the train used fifteen per cent less electricity while travelling ten per cent faster.

### Párrafo C

The appeal of such solutions is not merely aesthetic. Biological structures are the product of an extremely long process of trial and error, in which variations that wasted energy or failed under stress were eliminated. A tree trunk distributes mechanical load with a minimum of material because trees that did otherwise fell down and did not reproduce. Over hundreds of millions of years, this filtering has produced structures that are, in engineering terms, remarkably close to optimal for the conditions in which they developed. Nature has, in effect, already run the experiments that a design team would need decades to complete.

### Párrafo D

Enthusiasm should nevertheless be tempered. Biological solutions are optimised for the size at which they occur, and physical forces do not scale proportionally: an insect's wing works because air behaves almost like syrup at that scale, which is why early attempts to build flapping aircraft on a human scale failed so consistently. Living organisms also build from a narrow range of substances at ambient temperature, whereas engineers work with steel, ceramics and polymers. A structure that is elegant in bone may be impossible, or simply pointless, in titanium. Copying form without understanding the constraints that produced it rarely works.

### Párrafo E

What was once regarded as a curiosity is now firmly embedded in professional practice. More than forty universities worldwide offer dedicated courses in bio-inspired design, and several national research councils have created funding streams specifically for the field. Major manufacturers maintain in-house teams whose remit is to search the biological literature for relevant structures, and a number of professional bodies have begun to include biomimetic methods in their accreditation requirements for engineering degrees. The discipline has acquired the conferences, journals and career paths that mark an established area of research rather than a passing enthusiasm.

### 🎯 Las tres trampas, y por qué existen

**vii — *Early failures in aviation*, para el párrafo B**
B habla de un tren, no de aviación, y no describe ningún fracaso. La palabra *aircraft* sí aparece… pero **en el párrafo D**. Es la trampa de la palabra repetida en el párrafo equivocado.

**iv — *From niche interest to established field*, para el párrafo E** ← **la difícil**
Es tentadora y no es falsa: E **empieza** diciendo *"what was once regarded as a curiosity is now firmly embedded"*. Pero eso es **una sola oración**. Las otras cuatro hablan de universidades, financiación, equipos internos y acreditación — **apoyo institucional**.

> **Aplica el test de una frase:** iv se apoya en la primera oración; **vi** cubre el párrafo entero. Gana vi.

**ii — *The economic cost of research*, para el párrafo E**
E menciona *funding streams*, pero **financiar** no es lo mismo que **costar**. El párrafo no dice en ningún momento que la investigación sea cara. Es la misma lógica del *Not Given*: la palabra está, la idea no.
